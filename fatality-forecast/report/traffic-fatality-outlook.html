NHTSA FARS 1995–2024 · Forecast 2025–2029

US traffic deaths are expected to hold near 40,000 a year through 2029

We trained statistical and machine-learning models on 30 years of fatal-crash records and tested each one on how well it would have predicted the past. The blend of the most accurate models projects annual deaths roughly flat nationally, with a wide range of plausible outcomes, and forecasts every state and DC.

Deaths per mile driven tell the underlying story

Fatality rate per 100 million vehicle miles traveled (VMT). Cars got safer for decades, then the rate jumped in 2020 when emptier roads brought faster, riskier driving. It has fallen for three straight years but is still above the 2019 level.

This matters for the forecast. Miles driven keep rising about 1% a year. If the rate keeps sliding back toward its pre-pandemic level of about 1.1, deaths would fall below the forecast. If it levels off where it is now, rising mileage pushes deaths slightly up. The blend sits between those two paths.

How the models scored

Average forecast error (MAPE) when each model was trained on data up to a given year and asked to predict the following 1–5 years. We repeated this for every starting year from 2005 to 2019, which gave 15 test runs per model that include the 2008 recession and the 2020 pandemic jump. Lower is better.

The simple “same as last year” baseline is hard to beat, which is common for annual data with sudden shocks. Errors grow with distance: about 4% one year out, around 12–13% five years out. The blend matches the baseline for next year and does better three to five years ahead, which is the horizon you asked about.

What the forecast assumes

Limits to keep in mind

  • 2025 isn't final yet. NHTSA has not released the 2025 FARS file. Its early quarterly estimates may already point lower than this forecast, which only uses finalized annual files. Compare against those estimates when they are published.
  • Shocks aren't predictable. No model in the backtest saw the 2008 drop or the 2020 jump coming. The ranges reflect that history, so they are wide on purpose.
  • 2024 may be revised. NHTSA revises recent years as records are finalized. Counts here come from the current data files and can differ slightly from earlier press releases.
  • Policy and technology aren't modeled. Effects from automatic emergency braking mandates, enforcement changes or speed-camera programs show up only to the extent they appear in past trends.

Data and methods

Fatalities

NHTSA Fatality Analysis Reporting System (FARS), national annual files 1995–2024, downloaded from static.nhtsa.gov. Total deaths = sum of FATALS across every crash record in each year's accident file.

Explanatory data

Vehicle miles traveled (FHWA Traffic Volume Trends), US population (Census, via BEA), and the unemployment rate (BLS). All taken from the St. Louis Fed's FRED mirrors of the official series.

Models

Baseline; linear drift; damped-trend exponential smoothing; ARIMA chosen automatically by AIC; ARIMAX with miles driven and unemployment; rate-per-mile times miles; ridge regression; gradient-boosted trees on the rate per mile. All are Python, statsmodels and scikit-learn.

States

FARS deaths by state; state population (Census) and unemployment (BLS) via FRED. Six models per state, including one gradient-boosting model trained on all states together. The best three are blended, then scaled to the national forecast. Ranges come from each state's own backtest errors, partly pooled with the all-state average. Population after 2025 grows at each state's 2020–2025 pace. State miles driven are left out because FHWA publishes them only as yearly spreadsheets whose layout changes.

Blend and ranges

The three models with the lowest backtest error (not counting the baseline) are averaged, weighted by inverse error. Ranges come from the blend's own backtest errors at each horizon.

The NHTSA CrashAPI and nhtsa.gov block automated requests from cloud servers, so this analysis uses FARS bulk downloads. They are the same records the API serves.
